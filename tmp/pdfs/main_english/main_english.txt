   Comparative Benchmarking of Neural OCR Methods for
 Handwritten Word Recognition in Medical Prescription Images
     under Limited-Data and Computational Constraints
                                           Samuel F. Santos∗
                                 Universidade Estadual de Santa Cruz



                                                 Abstract
              This study presents a practical comparison of CNN-based and Transformer-based models
          for handwritten word recognition in medical prescription images. Two source datasets,
          RxHandBD and Doctor’s Handwritten PrescriptionBD, were evaluated individually and in a
          combined training scenario. From-scratch training was used for a CNN baseline formulated
          as a closed-set word classifier and for a CNN-BiLSTM sequence-recognition model, whereas
          the pre-trained TrOCR and PARSeq Transformers were fine-tuned for character-sequence
          transcription. The study examines how dataset characteristics, training regimes, and task
          formulations affect recognition performance, model limitations, and training cost under
          limited-data conditions.

Keywords: Neural Networks; Convolution; Transformers; Artificial Intelligence.


1        Introduction
During medical appointments, patients are often issued a medical prescription containing in-
formation about medications and treatment instructions intended to support their recovery.
However, the readability of these prescriptions is frequently affected by the physician’s handwrit-
ing and the context in which they are written. Factors such as the urgency of medical care and
the high demand for patient consultations can negatively impact the legibility of handwritten
prescriptions, making their interpretation more challenging for both healthcare professionals and
patients.
     As a consequence, medical prescriptions often present low legibility, making their interpreta-
tion challenging for both pharmacists and patients. This difficulty has led several countries to
adopt digital prescription systems as a way to reduce interpretation errors and improve patient
safety. However, this transition has not occurred universally, and handwritten prescriptions are
still widely used in many healthcare settings.
     Therefore, it is important to investigate and understand the challenges of applying Optical
Character Recognition (OCR) to handwritten medical prescriptions, including both textual
characters and medical symbols. Furthermore, it is relevant to evaluate whether the knowledge
and techniques developed for this domain can be generalized to other types of handwritten
documents.
     It is understood that handwritten manuscripts exhibit several characteristics relevant to
this study, as they are composed of simplified geometric shapes and structural patterns that
can be interpreted from the symbols present in the writing. Figures 1a and 1b illustrate these
fundamental shapes while also highlighting the presence of noise and irregularities resulting from
environmental conditions and the writing process, as previously discussed. Consequently, these

    In the context of neural networks, it is important to evaluate the ability of different ar-
chitectures to learn and recognize handwriting structures. This motivation is justified by the
possibility of automatically classifying medical prescriptions and by the potential to use these
structures as a basis for learning robust representations applicable to problems involving more
complex geometric patterns, as demonstrated in [1].
    The characteristics present in handwritten medical prescriptions are particularly relevant for
model training because they reflect the natural variability introduced during the handwriting
process. Furthermore, handwritten prescriptions incorporate variations arising from the writing
process, the writer’s individual style, and external influences. This differs from synthetic data,
which generally exhibit more controlled patterns and lower variability.
    Consequently, these characteristics play an important role in neural network training by
enabling models to learn more robust representations with greater generalization capability when
dealing with the variability found in real-world scenarios.
    However, neural network training using handwritten medical prescription data faces challenges
related to data scarcity, image quality, and annotation quality. Data scarcity results from the
limited availability of publicly available datasets for training, making it necessary to adopt data
augmentation techniques to increase the number and diversity of training samples. Regarding
image quality, this challenge is associated with the age of existing datasets, as older datasets
often contain images with lower resolution, reduced sharpness, and a higher incidence of artifacts.
These factors should be carefully considered during the data collection and preprocessing stages.
Annotation quality also represents an important challenge, as each dataset may exhibit limitations
associated with regional characteristics. Consequently, there is no guarantee that a dataset
collected in the United States, for example, shares the same handwriting patterns, terminology,
and structural characteristics as medical prescriptions written in Brazil or European countries.
These differences reflect linguistic, cultural, and healthcare system variations across regions. To
address these challenges, different strategies were adopted throughout this study to mitigate
their impact and improve both the quality of model training and the generalization capability of
the proposed neural networks.
    Despite the limitations related to data availability, this study does not aim to collect new
handwritten medical prescriptions or construct a novel dataset. Nevertheless, an adequate
number of training samples is required to effectively train the neural network models. To
address this limitation, data augmentation techniques are employed to increase data diversity

will be used independently, allowing the performance of the models to be evaluated on each
dataset separately. Subsequently, a new training process will be conducted using a combination
of both datasets. This strategy aims to increase the number of available training samples while

    The datasets selected for this study were RxHandBD [2] and PrescriptionBD [3], as they

augmentation techniques and organizing the training sets, the data will be ready to be used for

    To conduct these experiments, four neural network architectures were evaluated: two based
on Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and two based on Transformers. The convolutional
architectures selected were a CNN Baseline and a CNN-BiLSTM. They were chosen because
they require lower computational cost and are less dependent on large volumes of training data,
characteristics that are consistent with the data scarcity scenario addressed in this study.
    The Transformer-based architectures selected were TrOCR and PARSeq. Both models
were initialized from pre-trained weights and subsequently fine-tuned for the proposed task.
This approach leverages knowledge acquired during pre-training, reducing the need for large
task-specific datasets.
    The performance evaluation was conducted by considering the characteristics of each archi-
tecture. Accordingly, the CNN-based models were analyzed within the training from scratch
paradigm, whereas the Transformer-based models were evaluated under the fine-tuning paradigm.
Thus, the comparisons were performed within the context of each training strategy, avoiding
direct comparisons that could lead to misleading interpretations due to the inherent differences
between their training processes.

of the datasets influence the performance of the different architectures, helping to identify the
factors that improve or limit their recognition capabilities.
    Within this context, the objective of this study is to evaluate, under a controlled experimental
setting, four neural network architectures for handwritten medical prescription recognition.

2     Method
To ensure a fair comparison among all models, the same training configuration was adopted
for every architecture. This approach enabled a consistent and unbiased benchmark across all
evaluated models. To achieve this, a standardized training protocol was developed, in which all
architectures were trained using the same settings, including the number of epochs, learning rate,
and batch size. This protocol sought to minimize the influence of external factors on the training
process, ensuring that the observed differences in performance were predominantly attributable
to the characteristics of the evaluated architectures and datasets. Consequently, the comparison
became more consistent, reliable, and representative of each model’s actual performance.
    At the end of the training process, the obtained results were consolidated through different
evaluation procedures. To this end, tables containing the main performance metrics throughout
the training process were generated, and the best-performing model across all training epochs
was saved. Subsequently, this model was evaluated to obtain the final performance metrics,
enabling a consistent assessment of its generalization capability. This approach provided a
reliable basis for comparing the evaluated architectures.

2.1    Proposed Approach
The adopted methodology for comparing the models consisted of standardizing all experimental
stages to ensure equivalent evaluation conditions. First, the dataset was preprocessed, including
its organization and partitioning into training, validation, and test splits. Next, the models were
implemented in Python and trained using the same hyperparameters, ensuring that performance
differences were attributable to the evaluated architectures rather than training configurations.
Finally, the models were evaluated using the test set, enabling a systematic comparison of the
obtained results.

    1. Initializing the models: Each architecture was configured with its respective parameters
       and output vocabulary. For transformer-based models, pre-trained checkpoints were used
       as the initial weights, whereas the remaining architectures were initialized with their default
       settings.

    2. Data processing: The images were converted to grayscale, resized to a height of 64
       pixels and a maximum width of 512 pixels, while preserving the aspect ratio and applying
       padding when necessary. Text labels were normalized by removing excess whitespace
       and converting all characters to lowercase. The data were organized into fixed training,
       validation, and test sets.

    3. Optimization: Training was performed in batches using the AdamW optimizer. The loss
       function was defined according to the prediction strategy of each architecture, including
       cross-entropy loss, CTC loss, or a permutation-based loss. At the end of each epoch,
       performance on the validation set was monitored to select the best checkpoint.

    4. Evaluation: The best checkpoint selected on the validation set was applied exclusively
       to the test set. Performance was quantified using exact-match accuracy, Character Error
       Rate (CER), and Word Error Rate (WER), allowing both complete prediction correctness
       and character- and word-level errors to be assessed.

3.1   Datasets and Preprocessing
The Doctor’s Handwritten Prescription BD Dataset (PrescriptionBD) was developed by re-
searchers from Bangladesh to provide a public dataset for handwritten medical prescription
recognition [4]. Its development was motivated by the limited availability of public datasets for
training and evaluating handwriting recognition models in the medical domain. To construct
the dataset, real medical prescriptions from Bangladesh were collected, and individual words
were segmented and annotated. The dataset was then made publicly available, enabling its use
in research on optical character recognition (OCR), handwritten text recognition (HTR), and
artificial intelligence applications in healthcare.
    The construction of PrescriptionBD involved segmenting, labeling, and classifying words
extracted from medical prescriptions, resulting in an organized dataset suitable for training and
evaluating handwriting recognition models. In general, the samples have good visual quality
and sufficient sharpness to support feature extraction by deep learning architectures.
    Figure 2 presents samples from PrescriptionBD. The dataset contains images with variable
resolutions in both height and width, making it necessary to standardize them to a single
size before training the neural networks. In addition, PrescriptionBD provides an official data
split, which supports experiment reproducibility and consistent comparisons among different
approaches.

3.2     Computational Models
Four architectures were used in this study: a CNN baseline, a CNN-BiLSTM, TrOCR, and
PARSeq.

3.2.1    CNN Baseline
Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) are inspired by principles observed in the biological
visual system, particularly the studies of the visual cortex conducted by Hubel and Wiesel [5].
Although they do not faithfully reproduce how the human brain operates, these architectures
were conceived from the observation that the visual system can recognize complex patterns
through hierarchical combinations of simple visual features.
    In the experiments conducted by Hubel and Wiesel, neurons in the visual cortex were found
to respond selectively to specific stimuli such as edges, orientations, and contrasts. Visual infor-
mation processing was also observed to occur hierarchically, with higher-level neurons integrating
information extracted by simpler neurons to represent increasingly complex structures [5].

                                                 7
    Inspired by these principles, CNNs introduce local receptive fields through convolutional layers,
in which filters automatically learn patterns present in the images. As multiple convolutional
layers are stacked, hierarchical feature representations can be constructed, beginning with basic
elements such as edges and textures and progressing toward more complex patterns such as
shapes and objects. This organization has made CNNs one of the main architectures used in
computer vision and pattern recognition tasks.
    The conceptual foundations of modern CNN models can be traced to Fukushima’s work,
presented in Neocognitron: A Self-Organizing Neural Network Model for a Mechanism of Pattern
Recognition Unaffected by Shift in Position [6]. That study proposed a hierarchical architecture
inspired by the visual cortex, with layers responsible for progressive feature extraction and
invariance to small translations in the input patterns. Although the Neocognitron did not use
backpropagation training, its architectural principles helped establish the foundation for modern
CNNs.
    LeCun et al. [7] later consolidated this paradigm by presenting a convolutional architecture
trained with backpropagation for optical character recognition (OCR), demonstrating that
automatically learned convolutional filters could achieve high performance in pattern recognition
tasks. This work established a practical basis for the widespread adoption of CNNs in computer
vision problems.
    In this study, the CNN was used as a reference model (baseline), representing the starting
point for comparison among the evaluated architectures. It establishes an initial performance
level for traditional convolutional models in handwritten medical prescription recognition and
provides a basis for analyzing the gains obtained with more sophisticated architectures.
    However, the problem addressed in this work differs from a conventional image classification
task. The objective is to recognize complete words in prescriptions, which characterizes a
sequence recognition problem. A conventional CNN produces only one classification for each
input image, preventing it from directly predicting all characters that form a word in a single
inference.
    To address this limitation, the CNN baseline was formulated as a word classifier. In this
approach, each word in the training set is treated as a distinct class. The network output
therefore corresponds to a probability distribution over all known classes, and the word with
the highest probability is selected as the prediction. The model does not perform sequential
character recognition; instead, it directly estimates which previously learned word corresponds
to the input image.
    As an extension of the CNN baseline, an architecture incorporating a Bidirectional Long
Short-Term Memory (BiLSTM) layer was developed. This modification allows the model to
move beyond the classification of previously known words and recognize the character sequence
in the image, making it more suitable for handwritten text recognition tasks.

3.2.2    CNN BiLSTM
To improve recognition accuracy, a Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (BiLSTM) module
was incorporated into the CNN baseline. This architecture is a recurrent neural network capable
of modeling long-range sequential dependencies by processing a sequence in both the forward
and backward directions [8]. The model can therefore consider not only the current element but
also the context supplied by preceding and subsequent characters.
    The inclusion of the BiLSTM is motivated by the inherent sequential structure of handwritten
words. In a language, some characters are more likely to occur together, including common
alternations between consonants and vowels and the spelling patterns associated with each
word. By exploring these contextual dependencies, the BiLSTM complements the visual features
extracted by the CNN, allowing the classification process to consider both character appearance
and the sequential relationships among characters. The CNN-BiLSTM architecture was therefore
evaluated for its ability to improve handwritten medical prescription recognition relative to the
CNN baseline.
    After incorporating the BiLSTM, the recognition approach remained based on a predefined
set of classes. Although sequential modeling allowed better use of the context among characters,
the architecture remained limited to classifying words that belonged to classes known during
training. The problem of recognizing unseen words or words formed by arbitrary character
combinations therefore remained.
    To overcome this limitation, Transformer-based architectures were subsequently evaluated.
Unlike the class-based classification approach, these models perform recognition at the character-
sequence level and can therefore recognize words that were not present in the training set.

3.2.3    TrOCR
TrOCR (Transformer-based Optical Character Recognition) is an optical character recognition
model proposed by Li et al. [9] that uses an encoder-decoder architecture based entirely on
Transformers. The model employs a visual Transformer to extract representations from the
input image and a textual Transformer to generate the corresponding sequence of characters or
tokens, eliminating the need for traditional CNN-, RNN-, and CTC-based architectures. TrOCR
can also be pre-trained on large synthetic datasets and subsequently fine-tuned with labeled
data, with applications to printed, handwritten, and scene text.

3.2.4    PARSeq
PARSeq (Permuted Autoregressive Sequence) is a Transformer-based model developed for
text recognition in images, particularly in the context of Scene Text Recognition [10]. Unlike
conventional autoregressive models, which generate characters only from left to right, PARSeq
uses Permutation Language Modeling, allowing the model to learn different prediction orders
and explore bidirectional context during recognition. This approach combines autoregressive
inference, non-autoregressive inference, and iterative refinement within a single architecture,
providing a balance among accuracy, parameter count, and computational cost.

    The results in Figure 6 show that all models achieved their best performance on Prescrip-
tionBD. This pattern was not observed on RxHandBD, where most models performed worse,
except for the CNN baseline, which produced relatively more consistent results.
    This difference may be associated primarily with the visual characteristics of each dataset.
As illustrated in Figure 3, RxHandBD images contain thicker, more blurred, and less clearly
defined strokes, making it more difficult for both CNNs and Transformer-based models to extract
discriminative features. In contrast, Figure 2 shows that PrescriptionBD contains characters with
thinner, more continuous, and better-defined strokes, which may facilitate pattern identification
and the learning of more robust representations by the neural networks. The observed visual-
quality differences between the datasets are therefore consistent with the differences in model
performance during training and inference.
    The experiments on the combined dataset produced intermediate performance relative to
the two original datasets. This behavior was expected because the combined set includes both
the favorable characteristics of PrescriptionBD and the challenges present in RxHandBD. The
models were exposed to higher-quality examples as well as samples with greater visual complexity,
resulting in average performance between the two original scenarios.
    One notable result was that CNN-BiLSTM achieved a lower CER on RxHandBD than on
PrescriptionBD, as shown in Figure 7. This result indicates that, although RxHandBD was more
challenging for most of the evaluated models, CNN-BiLSTM recognized individual characters
more accurately in this specific scenario.
    Across all evaluated models and datasets, WER behaved consistently with overall accuracy,
as illustrated in Figure 8. In general, models with higher accuracy also had lower WER values,
showing the inverse relationship between these metrics and supporting the consistency of the
experimental results.

5    Limitations
This study has limitations related to the amount of available data and the training batch sizes,
which were restricted by the available memory capacity. These limitations may have introduced
some bias into the results. However, all models were trained and evaluated under the same
experimental conditions, using the same computational environment and training configurations,
to provide the fairest possible comparison among the methods.


6    Conclusion
The results demonstrate the potential of sequence-recognition models for transcribing handwritten
words in medical prescriptions. In particular, TrOCR and PARSeq achieved the best performance
among the evaluated methods, with consistent advantages over the CNN baseline in all three
experimental scenarios. PARSeq performed best on PrescriptionBD, reaching 90.13% accuracy,
a CER of 0.0680, and a WER of 0.1000, whereas TrOCR achieved the best overall performance
in the combined scenario in terms of accuracy (80.88%) and WER (0.2018). These results
indicate that pre-trained models adapted through fine-tuning are an effective strategy for OCR
of handwritten medical text, even under limited-data conditions.
